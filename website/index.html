Since 1974

Fifty years on Museum Avenue

We opened in 1974 with one hall and one dinosaur. Fifty years later we have three halls, a basement nobody has fully explored, and the same dinosaur. He's held up well.

To celebrate, there's a parade down Museum Avenue with floats, balloons, a marching band and a giant donut, and kids get in free all week.

Getting here

1 Museum Avenue. Bus 12 stops right outside the front steps. We don't have a parking lot; the nearest garage is two blocks east, behind City Savings Bank.

On parade day Museum Avenue is closed to cars from 9 AM and the sidewalk out front is for parade watching, so come in through the side door on the east of the building.

Getting around

Everything is on one floor, with step-free access from the side door. Wheelchairs and strollers can be borrowed at the information desk. Service animals are welcome. Other animals, sadly, are not.

House rules

  • Look with your eyes. The sand at the dig site is the only thing you're allowed to touch.
  • Photos are fine. No flash in the Egyptian Wing, the paint is 3,000 years old.
  • No food in the galleries. Yes, that includes donuts.
  • Please walk. The floors are marble and our insurance company is watching.
  • Don't knock on the sarcophagus.

The information desk

Right in the middle of the lobby. Tickets, maps, wheelchairs, lost property, and someone who knows where the bathrooms are.

The Museum Shop

In the lobby, next to the Roman Hall. Books, fossil casts, plush dinosaurs, rubber ducks, and every poster from every exhibition we've ever had. Open museum hours.

//Dinosaur Hall
Permanent exhibition · North wing

The Dinosaur Hall

252 to 66 million years ago, under three skylights. In the middle of it all is the King: our Tyrannosaurus rex, on the same steel mount since 1974.

it does NOT come alive at night. stop asking- front desk

About the hall

The Dinosaur Hall is our oldest gallery. The walls are big rough stone blocks, and a few of them have real fossils pressed into them. See how many you can find. Daylight comes in through three skylights 13 meters up, which is good, because the T. rex is 4 meters tall at the hip.

The skeleton is a cast made from the bones of several animals. It has stood on the same platform since the hall opened, and that's all we're going to say about that.

Don't miss

Tyrannosaurus rex

"Tyrant lizard king." About 12 meters long and 8 tonnes, with teeth the size of bananas. Its arms were tiny, with only two fingers on each hand. Please stop asking if it could do a push-up.

Triceratops

Three horns, a bony frill and a beak like a parrot. Ate plants all day and still weighed as much as an elephant. Its name is Gerald, according to a sticky note on its plaque.

Velociraptor

About the size of a turkey, and covered in feathers. The movies made them a lot bigger.

Iron meteorite

A piece of an asteroid, about 4.5 billion years old. Older than everything else in the building, including the plumbing. Please stop licking it.

Ammonites

Sea animals with coiled shells, related to squid. They look a lot like cinnamon rolls. Do not eat them.

Dig site

Real sand, cast bones. Ages 4 to 12. Grown-ups, let the kids dig.

Family activity

Crack the code

Next to the T. rex is an old service panel, the Dino-Lock 3000. Somebody scratched a message into it in a simple code:

//Egyptian Wing
Brand new wing

The Egyptian Wing

Carved sandstone, a midnight-blue ceiling covered in gold stars, and a sarcophagus about 3,000 years old. The lid stays on.

DO NOT knock on the lid- Aaron

About the wing

Our newest gallery opened this year, between the Dinosaur Hall and the Roman Hall. The walls are sandstone blocks with a band of hieroglyphs around the top. The four painted pillars each show a sign and a number, written the way the ancient Egyptians wrote numbers. Somebody is going to make a puzzle out of that one day.

Don't miss

The sarcophagus

Painted blue and gold. Mummies were wrapped in hundreds of meters of linen, and this one has never been unwrapped. We'd like to keep it that way.

Anubis

Three statues of the jackal-headed god who looked after the dead. One is off its stand. We're not sure how.

Canopic jars

Four jars with lids shaped like a jackal, a falcon, a baboon and a human. You don't want to know what was inside.

The ankh

The Egyptian sign for life. It looks like a key. It is not a key. The night shift checked.

Scarab beetle

Scarabs roll balls of dung across the sand. Egyptians thought a giant beetle rolled the sun across the sky the same way. Kids find this either amazing or gross.

Sphinx and obelisk

Small copies of two very large things.

The Roman Empire

Red painted walls like a house in Pompeii, a gold pattern running around the top, a coffered ceiling, and a mosaic floor that somebody laid one tiny tile at a time.

the chariot is NOT for sitting in. looking at you, Tyler- staff

About the hall

Eight white columns run the length of the Roman Hall. At the far end, between two SPQR banners, is the museum's foundation tablet. It says MVSEVM CONDITVM ANNO MCMLXXIV, which means "the museum, founded in the year 1974." The Romans didn't have the letter U, so they used V. It looks weird. That's how you know it's real.

The emperors

Six marble busts line the hall: Julius Caesar, Augustus, Nero, Marcus Aurelius, Hadrian, and .

Don't miss

Racing chariot

Pulled by two or four horses around the Circus Maximus at about 40 km/h. No seatbelts.

Gladiator's shield

Gladiators fought in front of crowds of 50,000, and the best ones had fans who wrote about them on walls. A visitor's dad could apparently beat this one.

The Orator and the Unknown Philosopher

Two marble statues. We know who one of them is.

Model of the Colosseum

1:200 scale. Please don't put your action figures in it.

Roman numerals, quickly

I is 1, V is 5, X is 10, L is 50, C is 100, D is 500, M is 1000. Add them up, biggest first: XVI is 10 + 5 + 1 = 16. A small one in front of a bigger one gets taken away: IV is 4, XL is 40, CM is 900. So MCMLXXIV is 1000 + 900 + 70 + 4 = 1974.

Behind the scenes

The collection

We look after about 1.2 million objects. Fewer than one in a hundred are on display. The rest are in the archives, the storage under and behind the galleries. Nobody has a complete map.

if you find the end of the archives, please tell us- Kevin, IT

Search the collection

Look something up by name, gallery or catalog number. Catalog numbers start with the year the object arrived.

Can I visit the archives?

No. They're closed to the public and to most of the staff. Researchers can ask for an appointment. The storage is split into sections, starting with Receiving, Lost and Found, Posters and Prints, Spare Dinosaur Bones, Egyptian Overflow and Broken Statues. After that it gets vague.

/About
Our story

Fifty years on Museum Avenue

A natural history museum for the city, since 1974. Run by people who really like old things, and guarded at night by people who really like donuts.

the T. rex has worked here longer than anyone- Brenda, gift shop

The building

It's a big neoclassical building in pale limestone. The middle has four columns, the museum's name carved across the top, and a triangle on the roof with a giant ammonite in the middle and a long-necked dinosaur on each side. Over the front doors, a plaque says EST. MCMLXXIV.

Two long wings stretch out on each side, with tall windows and a banner for every gallery: green for the dinosaurs, gold for Egypt, red for Rome, and blue for the birthday. Out front there are planters, benches, and the bronze triceratops everybody climbs on.

Inside, the lobby has a black-and-white marble floor, four marble columns and a fancy ceiling eight meters up. The Dinosaur Hall is to the north, the Roman Hall to the east, and the security office to the west, where the night guard definitely does not watch videos all night.
